# Training Book Principles

Use this reference when generating marathon, half marathon, 5K/10K, strength, or cross-training plans. It summarizes public information and widely known coaching frameworks from the requested books. Do not quote book text. Treat these as coaching heuristics, then adapt to COROS recovery, HRV, sleep, training load, injury signals, and the runner's actual base.

## How to Use These Books Together

- Use **Hal Higdon / Marathon: The Ultimate Training Guide** and **Runner's World Big Book of Marathon and Half-Marathon Training** for accessible beginner-to-intermediate structure.
- Use **Hansons Marathon Method** when the runner can train frequently and benefits from cumulative fatigue, marathon-pace work, and distributed weekly stress.
- Use **Advanced Marathoning** when the runner has an established mileage base and wants a performance-oriented marathon plan.
- Use **Daniels' Running Formula** to choose individualized training paces from current fitness, not goal fantasy pace.
- Use **Heart Rate Training** when pace is unreliable because of heat, hills, fatigue, or variable terrain.
- Use **The Secret of Running** when the runner has reliable running power data or asks for course-adjusted effort modeling.
- Use **NSCA tactical strength** and **High-Performance Training for Sports** for strength, power, mobility, monitoring, and durability decisions.
- Never stack the hardest interpretation of several systems at once. If combining sources, preserve one primary plan architecture and borrow only small, compatible elements from the others.

## Book Map

### Hansons Marathon Method / 汉森训练法

Core idea:

- Build marathon readiness through **cumulative fatigue**: repeated manageable stress across the week, not a single heroic long run.
- Prioritize frequent running, controlled quality, marathon-specific tempo, and enough easy volume to make the late miles feel familiar.
- The signature hard sessions are often called "SOS" sessions: speed, strength, tempo, and long run. In this context, "strength" means longer aerobic running repetitions, not gym lifting.

Useful training types:

- Easy runs and recovery runs.
- Speed intervals early in a cycle for economy and turnover.
- Strength intervals later in a cycle: longer controlled repetitions near marathon-specific effort.
- Marathon-pace tempo runs.
- Long runs that fit inside the weekly load rather than dominating the week.

Best use:

- Marathon runners who can handle 5-6 days per week and already tolerate steady mileage.
- Runners who fade late in races and need specific fatigue resistance.

Be careful:

- Do not use the full cumulative-fatigue structure for injury-prone, low-mileage, or 3-day-per-week runners.
- COROS red flags matter: high load ratio, low recovery, poor sleep, HRV suppression, or local pain should reduce the next SOS session first.

### Runner's World Big Book of Marathon and Half-Marathon Training / 马拉松训练宝典

Core idea:

- Treat this as a broad toolkit for many runner levels rather than a single strict doctrine.
- Match plan difficulty to experience, available days, race distance, and goal.
- Include the surrounding practices that make training sustainable: injury prevention, fueling, shoes/gear, recovery, and race execution.

Useful training types:

- Easy/base runs.
- Long runs.
- Tempo or steady-state runs.
- Race-pace work.
- Hills.
- Speedwork or track-style sessions.
- Cross-training.
- Strength, mobility, and rest days.

Best use:

- First-time to intermediate runners who need clear structure and practical race preparation.
- Half-marathon and marathon planning when the user wants a balanced, approachable plan.

Be careful:

- Because it is a toolkit, do not include every workout type every week. Pick the minimum set that serves the goal and recovery state.
- In base-phase week 1 for recreational runners, choose easy running, long run progression, and strength/cross-training before adding formal fartlek, tempo, or intervals.

### Advanced Marathoning / 高级马拉松训练

Core idea:

- Marathon performance depends on aerobic development, glycogen management, lactate threshold, running economy, VO2max, durability, and a disciplined taper.
- Use periodized marathon plans with substantial aerobic volume, long runs, medium-long runs, and targeted quality sessions.
- Tune-up races can be useful, but they must not derail the marathon build.

Useful training types:

- Recovery runs.
- General aerobic runs.
- Medium-long runs.
- Long runs, sometimes with marathon-pace segments.
- Lactate-threshold or tempo work.
- VO2max intervals.
- Speed/form work.
- Hill work.
- Tune-up races.
- Doubles, only for runners who already handle enough volume.
- Strength and core support.

Best use:

- Experienced runners with a stable weekly mileage base and a performance marathon goal.
- Runners who can recover from higher volume and tolerate 12-18+ week builds.

Be careful:

- Do not prescribe advanced mileage, doubles, or dense quality blocks to beginners.
- If COROS shows load spikes or poor recovery, remove VO2max and threshold stress before removing all easy aerobic work.

### Marathon: The Ultimate Training Guide / 马拉松终极训练指南

Core idea:

- Make marathon training approachable through gradual progression, consistency, long-run development, rest, and simple plan levels.
- The plan should fit the runner's life. For many beginners, finishing healthy is a better first target than complex pace work.

Useful training types:

- Easy runs.
- Long-run progression.
- Rest days.
- Cross-training.
- Marathon-pace or pace-awareness work in more advanced plans.
- Tempo, hills, or speedwork only when appropriate for experience.

Best use:

- Beginners, first-marathon runners, and busy recreational runners.
- Cases where the user needs a simple plan that is easy to follow manually.

Be careful:

- Do not overcomplicate a novice plan with too many intensity zones.
- If the runner is undertrained near race day, prioritize safe completion and pacing strategy over late fitness gains.

### Daniels' Running Formula / 丹尼尔斯经典跑步训练法

Core idea:

- Use current race performance or current fitness to estimate training ability, then prescribe paces by workout purpose.
- Every workout should have a clear physiological purpose. Avoid turning easy days into medium-hard days.
- Classic Daniels intensity families: E, M, T, I, and R.

Useful training types:

- **E pace**: easy aerobic running and recovery.
- **M pace**: marathon-pace running for specificity and pacing.
- **T pace**: threshold work, including continuous tempo and cruise intervals.
- **I pace**: interval work aimed around VO2max development.
- **R pace**: repetition work for speed, mechanics, and economy with generous recovery.

Best use:

- Choosing paces from recent race results, time trials, or reliable threshold estimates.
- Preventing the common mistake of training every run too hard.

Be careful:

- Use current fitness, not desired race result, to set paces.
- Adjust paces for heat, hills, altitude, surface, and fatigue.
- For very short repetitions, HR is too delayed to guide intensity; use pace, power, or perceived effort.
- In early base training, use E running and occasional relaxed R-style touches only when fresh; do not turn the first week into a hidden I/T workout.

### Heart Rate Training

Core idea:

- Heart rate is an internal-load signal. It helps separate easy, aerobic, threshold, and high-intensity work when pace alone is misleading.
- Zones should be individualized from tested max HR, threshold HR, resting HR, or observed field data when possible.
- HR should guide effort trends, not override every subjective signal.

Useful training types:

- Recovery runs at low HR.
- Easy aerobic endurance with HR caps.
- Long runs with controlled cardiac drift.
- Sub-threshold steady running.
- Threshold intervals.
- High-intensity intervals when HR response is meaningful.
- Cross-training by HR when running impact needs to be reduced.

Best use:

- Hot, humid, hilly, windy, or trail conditions.
- Runners who go too hard on easy days.
- COROS users with reliable HR history and zones.

Be careful:

- Wrist HR can be noisy; prefer trend and context over single readings.
- HR lags during short intervals and can be affected by heat, dehydration, caffeine, stress, poor sleep, and illness.
- If pace, HR, RPE, and recovery disagree, choose the more conservative reading.

### The Secret of Running

Core idea:

- Running performance can be understood quantitatively through power, energy cost, running economy, critical power/sustainable power, and environmental resistance.
- Pace is an output. Effort can change dramatically with wind, slope, surface, fatigue, and weather.
- Power data can help normalize effort across hills and wind when the sensor is reliable.

Useful training types:

- Easy and endurance runs by power range.
- Threshold or critical-power work.
- Intervals prescribed by power and duration.
- Hill sessions where power controls effort better than pace.
- Performance testing to update power-duration estimates.
- Race modeling for target power, pacing, and terrain.

Best use:

- Runners using Stryd or another reliable running-power source.
- Hilly or windy courses where pace targets are misleading.
- Data-oriented runners who want pacing decisions tied to physics and physiology.

Be careful:

- Power models are aids, not truth machines. Validate with RPE, HR, splits, and race outcomes.
- Do not transfer cycling power assumptions directly to running.
- Sensor calibration and shoe/device changes can affect continuity.

### NSCA's Essentials of Tactical Strength and Conditioning

Note: the public Human Kinetics page checked for the 2nd edition on 2026-05-18 listed a release date of 2026-06-30. Use this section as a high-level synthesis from the public catalog, table of contents, and established NSCA tactical-strength framework, not as a chapter-by-chapter substitute for the book.

Core idea:

- Tactical athletes need broad readiness: strength, power, endurance, agility, mobility, load carriage, resilience, and recovery.
- Start from a needs analysis, then periodize training around the athlete's mission, weak links, injury history, and available recovery.
- For road runners, the main value is durability and force-production support, not turning marathon training into tactical training.

Useful training types:

- Maximal and submaximal strength.
- Power and plyometrics.
- Sprint, agility, and change-of-direction work.
- Aerobic and anaerobic conditioning.
- Load carriage, when relevant.
- Mobility, warm-up, cooldown, and recovery.
- Testing and monitoring.

Best use:

- Runners who need strength resilience, carry-load ability, obstacle/military/police/fire demands, or return-to-training structure.
- Off-season or base-phase strength blocks.

Be careful:

- Heavy lower-body lifting and plyometrics can compromise key run workouts if poorly timed.
- Avoid new tactical-strength stress close to an A race.
- For pure road-running goals, use a minimum effective dose: 1-2 focused strength sessions per week in build phases, lighter maintenance near races.

### High-Performance Training for Sports

Core idea:

- Build performance through an integrated system: movement quality, strength, power, speed, conditioning, monitoring, recovery, and interdisciplinary planning.
- Training qualities should support the sport's demands and the athlete's current limiting factors.
- Monitoring and feedback loops matter: plan, observe response, adapt.

Useful training types:

- Movement preparation.
- Mobility and stability.
- Strength development.
- Power and plyometrics.
- Speed and acceleration mechanics.
- Change of direction, when relevant.
- Conditioning.
- Recovery and regeneration.
- Monitoring, testing, and return-to-play decisions.

Best use:

- Designing runner strength and athletic-development add-ons.
- Managing concurrent training without letting gym work sabotage run quality.
- Building a more complete athlete in base phases or off-season periods.

Be careful:

- Road race specificity still comes from running. Use gym and athletic work to support the run plan, not compete with it.
- Reduce complexity for recreational runners. Pick the few interventions that solve a clear problem.

## Training Type Library

Use this library to translate the books into concrete plans.

### Easy / Recovery Run

- Purpose: aerobic volume, recovery, mitochondrial and capillary support, habit formation.
- Use often. Most weekly running should be easy.
- Downgrade or replace with walk, swim, bike, or rest when COROS recovery, HRV, sleep, pain, or load ratio is poor.

### Aerobic Endurance / General Aerobic Run

- Purpose: build aerobic durability beyond recovery pace but below threshold.
- Use in base and marathon builds.
- Keep it controlled; do not let this become hidden tempo work.

### Long Run

- Purpose: musculoskeletal durability, glycogen/fueling practice, mental rehearsal, and late-race fatigue resistance.
- Progress from current longest recent run; never jump because a plan template says so.
- Add marathon-pace segments only for prepared runners with good recovery.

### Medium-Long Run

- Purpose: extra aerobic endurance without the stress of the longest run.
- Useful for advanced marathoners.
- Avoid for low-mileage runners unless total weekly load supports it.

### Marathon Pace / Race Pace

- Purpose: pacing skill, economy at target effort, race-specific confidence.
- Use for marathon and half-marathon specificity.
- Set by current ability and course demands, not only the dream goal.

### Threshold / Tempo

- Purpose: improve sustainable hard effort and lactate clearance.
- Can be continuous tempo or cruise intervals.
- Keep controlled; it should not become an all-out time trial unless explicitly testing.

### Interval / VO2max

- Purpose: raise high-end aerobic capacity and improve pace tolerance.
- Use sparingly in marathon plans and more often in 5K/10K blocks.
- Remove first when recovery is poor or race week is close.

### Repetition / Short Sprint Touches

- Purpose: economy, mechanics, neuromuscular sharpness.
- Use relaxed 15-20 second touches after easy runs when healthy, or structured short reps with full recovery for advanced plans.
- Stop before form gets forced. In Chinese output, call relaxed touches `短时间冲刺跑`, not `strides`.

### Hills

- Purpose: strength endurance, mechanics, power, and controlled intensity.
- Use hill sprints, hill repeats, or rolling aerobic runs depending on goal.
- Avoid hard downhill stress when injury risk or soreness is high.

### Strength / Mobility

- Purpose: durability, tendon and tissue capacity, force production, posture, and economy support.
- Base/build: 1-2 focused sessions weekly if the runner tolerates it.
- Race week: activation, mobility, core, calf/foot maintenance only.

### Cross-Training

- Purpose: aerobic work with lower impact or recovery support.
- Use cycling, swimming, elliptical, rowing, brisk walking, or mobility depending on pain and fatigue.
- Do not pretend cross-training is identical to running; describe why it is included.

### Testing / Tune-Up Race

- Purpose: update current fitness, paces, HR zones, or power targets.
- Use time trials, tune-up races, threshold checks, or power-duration tests.
- Do not test when the runner is injured, sick, under-recovered, or in the final days before an A race.

## Source Priority Rules

1. Health and injury constraints override every book.
2. COROS recovery, HRV, sleep, resting HR, load ratio, and pain reports override template workouts.
3. Current fitness sets intensity. Goal race time sets direction but not daily workout paces.
4. Beginner plans should prefer Higdon/Runner's World style simplicity.
5. Advanced marathon plans may borrow from Hansons, Advanced Marathoning, and Daniels, but only one hard architecture should dominate.
6. HR and power are measurement systems. Use them to refine effort; do not let them erase RPE and symptoms.
7. Strength systems support running. Keep soreness and interference low during race-specific blocks.
