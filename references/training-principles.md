# Training Principles

These rules guide plan generation. They are not rigid formulas; adapt to the athlete, race date, recovery, injury risk, and available terrain.

## Intensity Distribution

- Most endurance training should be easy enough to recover from.
- For recreational runners, a practical target is roughly 75-85% easy aerobic work and 15-25% moderate/hard work across a normal build phase.
- Avoid stacking hard days. Separate demanding workouts with easy or rest days.
- In the final week before a long race, keep only small controlled intensity touches.
- For many easy-run days, add relaxed short sprint touches at the end when the runner is healthy: usually 4-8 x 15-20 seconds fast but smooth, with full walking or easy-jog recovery. These are neuromuscular reminders, not sprints to exhaustion. In Chinese output, call them `短时间冲刺跑`, not `strides`.

## Progressive Load

- Build volume gradually from the runner's actual recent baseline.
- Use weekly mileage caps by recent baseline: below 20 km may increase up to 30% only for true beginners with good recovery; 20-30 km should usually increase by no more than 20%; above 30 km should usually increase by no more than 10%.
- Avoid increasing weekly volume, long-run duration, and intensity at the same time.
- Use cutback or recovery weeks after harder blocks.
- If current training history is unknown, choose a conservative plan and ask for recent weekly volume.

## Periodization and Weekly Structure

- Complete race cycles should not repeat the same week template. Use stages such as aerobic base, intensity development, race-specific consolidation, and taper.
- In the first week of an aerobic base phase, avoid hard fartlek, threshold, tempo, VO2max intervals, and race-pace workouts unless recent data clearly shows the runner is already adapted to that workload. Prefer easy running, long-run rhythm, strength/cross-training, and short relaxed sprint touches.
- For office workers, default Monday to rest unless the user requests otherwise. Do not add default flexibility/mobility work to Monday rest days.
- Put long runs on Saturday or Sunday by default, usually Sunday when the user does not specify.
- Keep most weekly distance easy: for many plans, easy runs plus the long run should make up roughly 75-85% of weekly mileage.
- Include only one or two hard running stimuli per week for most recreational runners, counting threshold, tempo, intervals, hard hills, and race-pace long-run segments.

## Long Runs

- Long runs build durability, fueling practice, and confidence.
- For half marathons and marathons, long runs should fit the athlete's history; do not create a sudden huge long run.
- Road-running plans should prioritize aerobic durability, controlled quality sessions, and repeatable recovery.
- Treadmill running is acceptable when outdoor running is impractical; use 0-1% incline for normal road substitution unless a specific workout calls for otherwise.

## Taper

- Taper reduces fatigue while preserving readiness.
- Reduce volume more than intensity; keep short, controlled race-pace or stride work if the athlete is healthy.
- Do not add new workouts, shoes, strength exercises, or terrain stress late in race week.
- For long races, freshness beats squeezing in one last large session.

## Strength Training

- Strength can support running economy, durability, and injury resistance.
- Use heavy or challenging lower-body strength in base/build periods, not immediately before A races.
- In race week, limit strength to mobility, activation, light core, and calf/foot maintenance.
- Stop or downgrade strength work if the runner feels unusual muscle soreness, obvious weakness, sharp pain, or movement quality getting worse.

## Recovery and Autoregulation

Downgrade the plan when any of these appear:

- HRV below normal or a sudden multi-day drop.
- Resting HR unusually elevated.
- Sleep short or poor quality.
- Recovery score low.
- Localized pain, unusual muscle soreness, obvious weakness, illness, fever, or unusual fatigue.
- Training load ratio high or recent large spike.

Prefer the smallest useful workout on questionable days: easy run, swim, walk, mobility, or rest.

## Cross-Training

- Use swimming, cycling, elliptical, brisk walking, or mobility as low-impact aerobic work.
- Cross-training can replace easy runs when the runner has soreness, joint irritation, poor sleep, or high life stress.
- Keep cross-training easy during recovery weeks and race weeks.
- Do not count all cross-training as equal to running volume; explain its purpose instead of converting aggressively.

## Easy Run Plus Short Sprint Touches

Use this pattern often in road-running plans, especially base building, 5K-10K work, and race-week sharpening:

1. Easy aerobic running first.
2. Then 4-8 short relaxed sprint touches of 15-20 seconds.
3. Recover fully between sprint touches; breathing and form should settle before the next one.
4. Stop if form tightens, pain appears, or fatigue turns the sprint touch into an all-out sprint.

Do not use short sprint touches after every run. Avoid them after long/hard workouts, during injury flare-ups, or when the user reports poor sleep and heavy legs.

## Source Notes

- Seiler and related endurance training literature supports large amounts of low-intensity work with limited high-intensity work for endurance athletes.
- Tapering research generally supports reducing training volume while maintaining some intensity before competition.
- Strength-training reviews in distance runners report benefits for running economy and performance when integrated without compromising endurance recovery.
- HRV-guided endurance training research supports using HRV as one signal among several, not as a standalone decision maker.
