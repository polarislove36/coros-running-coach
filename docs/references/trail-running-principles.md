# Trail Running Training Principles

This reference converts concepts from ultrarunning and uphill-athlete training sources into original coaching rules for this project. Do not reproduce long passages from the source books.

Source books reviewed:

- Jason Koop, *Training Essentials for Ultrarunning*.
- Steve House, Scott Johnston, and Kilian Jornet, *Training for the Uphill Athlete*.

## Planning Hierarchy

For long trail and ultrarunning goals, plan from the target race backward:

- Identify the race distance, elevation gain/loss, surface, altitude, weather, aid-station spacing, cutoffs, and expected finish time.
- Put the most race-specific work closest to the race, after a general base and build period.
- Use tune-up races as training only when they fit the long-range plan and do not compromise recovery.
- Do not require an arbitrary "magic" longest run distance. Use time-on-feet, accumulated vertical, fueling practice, and recovery response to judge readiness.

For an A race, later B/C races or other sports should support the A-race preparation rather than compete with it.

## Event Demand Modeling

Trail planning should estimate:

- Distance and expected duration.
- Elevation gain and loss.
- Average vertical per kilometer.
- Technical terrain and hiking percentage.
- Heat, altitude, night running, gear, aid spacing, and crew/pacer constraints.

For hilly ultras, use elevation as a core training target, not a note. A practical weekly goal is to gradually approximate the race's vertical density when possible, especially in the final specific weeks. If outdoor vertical is unavailable, use incline treadmill, stairs, step-ups, and specific strength as substitutes while acknowledging that downhill tolerance remains undertrained.

## Intensity Control

Use effort, heart rate, ventilation, and terrain context more than flat-road pace.

- Most ultra and mountain-running training should be easy aerobic work.
- Use intensity sparingly and intentionally. More intensity is not a substitute for missing aerobic durability.
- On steep climbs, hiking can be the correct race-specific choice.
- On descents, do not chase heart rate zones. Heart rate may drop while muscular damage still accumulates.
- Do not prescribe trail workouts by road pace unless the route is flat and comparable.

## Key Workout Families

### Easy Aerobic / Endurance

Purpose: build the base that supports all later specific work.

- Mostly conversational effort.
- Can be run, hike-run, incline treadmill, or low-impact aerobic cross-training.
- If fatigue is high, preserve frequency but reduce duration.

### Uphill Aerobic Strength

Purpose: improve sustainable climbing without excessive impact.

- Use steady uphill intervals, incline treadmill, stairs, or hiking with poles/pack when relevant.
- Keep the first weeks controlled; muscular endurance and tendon tolerance adapt more slowly than breathing.
- Downhill recovery should be easy and controlled.

### Muscular Endurance / Specific Strength

Purpose: improve force production and durability in sport-like movement.

- Use step-ups, lunges, split squats, loaded uphill hiking, stairs, or treadmill incline.
- Avoid adding heavy lower-body strength and big vertical increases in the same week.
- Stop or downgrade when knee, Achilles, back, or unusual quad soreness appears.

### Downhill Tolerance

Purpose: prepare the quads and connective tissue for eccentric load.

- A small amount goes a long way.
- Progress descents carefully and separate them from heavy strength or long downhill races.
- When the athlete lacks outdoor descents, use eccentric strength, step-downs, and cautious race-specific exposure when available.
- Do not use downhill intervals as a default fitness workout.

### Long Trail / Hike-Run

Purpose: time-on-feet, fueling, gear, hiking strategy, and mental rhythm.

- Progress from recent long-duration history, not only distance.
- Practice race-day carbohydrate, fluid, sodium, gear, lighting, poles, shoes, socks, and pack.
- Use long sessions to test problems, not to prove toughness.

## Monitoring and Fatigue

Combine objective and subjective data:

- COROS recovery, HRV, sleep, resting HR, load ratio, and training load.
- Local soreness, especially quads after downhill, calves/Achilles after climbing, and feet after long technical terrain.
- Mood, motivation, appetite, and sleep quality.

If load ratio is high or soreness persists, remove intensity first, then reduce vertical, then reduce duration.

For trail athletes, normal HR can hide muscular damage. Soreness and movement quality are critical.

## Terrain Substitution

When mountains are unavailable:

- Incline treadmill can substitute for steady climbing and aerobic uphill work.
- Stairs can substitute for hiking/climbing strength but should be progressed carefully.
- Step-ups and loaded carries can support muscular endurance.
- Cycling can add aerobic volume with low impact, but it does not replace downhill tolerance or foot durability.
- The plan should clearly label the missing stimulus: outdoor descending and technical trail skill.

## Fueling

Long trail plans must train the gut.

- Fuel from early in long sessions.
- Use the athlete's known carbohydrate tolerance as the upper bound, not the default for every session.
- Practice fluid concentration, sodium, and solid-food options under realistic intensity and heat.
- If the athlete cannot fuel a long session, downgrade intensity and duration.

## Taper and Race Strategy

Before an A ultra:

- Reduce volume more than frequency.
- Keep short touches of climbing rhythm but avoid new eccentric downhill exposure.
- No new shoes, pack, poles, foods, or aggressive strength work.
- Use a written race plan: pacing, hiking triggers, aid-station routine, carb/fluid/sodium targets, night strategy, and problem-solving rules.
